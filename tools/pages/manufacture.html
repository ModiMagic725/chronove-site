Precision, in five stages

How a CHRONOVÉ would be made: from a bar of steel to a watch that runs within a few seconds a day.

Slow on purpose

A calibre has between 150 and 300 parts. Most are smaller than a grain of rice, and many are cut to a tolerance of a few microns, a fraction of the width of a hair.

The workshop moves at the speed of the hardest step. Bridges are chamfered by hand, wheels are polished one at a time, and no watch leaves the bench until it has run for three full days in six positions.

The film below was shot at the movement bench. Watch the balance for a moment; it is making eight beats a second.

The five stages

{{scene:manufacture-stage-1}}

Component making

Plates, bridges and wheels are cut from bar stock and brass, then deburred. Tolerances are measured in microns.

2

microns, a typical tolerance

{{scene:manufacture-stage-2}}

Finishing

Edges are bevelled and polished, surfaces are brushed or grained. About thirty hours go into one skeleton movement.

30

hours to finish one skeleton calibre

{{scene:manufacture-stage-3}}

Assembly

Under magnification, a watchmaker places each jewel, wheel and bridge, and oils the pivots with a single drop.

187

parts in calibre CV-01

{{scene:manufacture-stage-4}}

Regulation

The balance spring is adjusted until the watch keeps time in six positions, hot and cold.

6

positions tested for rate

{{scene:manufacture-stage-5}}

Quality control

Water resistance, shock tests and a 72-hour rate check. A watch that fails goes back to the bench.

72

hours of rate checking
